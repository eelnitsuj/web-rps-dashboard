The lead metric. Revenue ÷ sessions collapses conversion and order value into one number, and it is the figure the traffic-quality debate is actually about. Weeks are Monday-start, matching Shopify's buckets.

Where the August 13 session drop came from

Daily sessions by referrer. When the Escape Hatch was removed on Aug 13, the drop landed almost entirely in the direct / unattributed bucket — not in Instagram, which barely moved.

Verdicts on the three open questions

Refuted
The pre-July baseline was 3.2%, not the 2.5–3% the owner remembers.

Measured sitewide conversion for Jun 8 – Jul 5 is 3.16%; the two spot weeks check out at 3.18% (Jun 22) and 3.44% (Jun 29). No day in the whole June baseline fell below 2.65%. The measured numbers are right and the remembered range is low — which means the decline is larger than the owner currently believes, not smaller.

Refuted
AOV is up ~6.5%, not the +26% implied by the −40% ÷ −52% arithmetic.

Aligning both metrics to the same window and the same channel (Online Store, week of Aug 3 vs. the June baseline): RPS −41.6%, CVR −45.1%, AOV +6.5%. Those three reconcile exactly (0.549 × 1.065 = 0.585). The +26% came from dividing an RPS and a CVR measured over different windows.

On Shopify's own average_order_value metric the lift reads larger — $51.40 → $57.42, about +13.8% — because that metric excludes post-order adjustments. Either way it is well short of +26%, and it has been fading since late July.

Confirmed, with a corrected cause
Phantom sessions were ~19% of traffic — but they were direct, not Instagram.

Day-of-week-matched Thu–Sat, the last pre-removal window (Aug 6–8) against the first clean one (Aug 13–15): total sessions 23,460 → 19,108, −18.5%. So the ~20% estimate holds on magnitude.

The attribution does not. Of the 4,352 sessions lost, 3,571 (82%) were direct / unattributed; Facebook gave up 686 and Instagram only 352 — a 4.4% dip, inside its normal week-to-week drift (Instagram ran 8,082 → 7,918 → 7,566 across the three Thu–Sat windows, a gentle slope with no step at Aug 13). The "3,731 → 2,357" reading compares a single Instagram peak day (Aug 11) against a trough, not a like-for-like window.

Consequence: stripping ~19% of phantom sessions raises the Aug 6–8 conversion rate from 2.09% to 2.57% — real, but it closes only about a third of the gap to the 3.22% June baseline. Most of the conversion decline survives the correction.

The same site is five different businesses, depending on who walks in

Conversion rate by traffic source, mobile only. Sitewide conversion averages these together and hides the thing that matters: the site performs for people who already know us and falls apart in front of strangers. Strangers are 69% of the traffic.

Caveat on the direct bucket: this window runs to Aug 16, so roughly 27 of its 30 days still contain the phantom sessions removed with the Escape Hatch on Aug 13. Direct is overstated here — which if anything widens the paid/owned gap rather than narrowing it.

The blockage is add-to-cart, not checkout

Add-to-cart rate by source — the leading indicator, and the thing the page changes below are meant to move. Sitewide, of the people who do add to cart, 81% go on to reach checkout. The page is not failing to persuade people who engage; it is failing to get anyone to engage. Turn on the data table for the paid funnel in absolute numbers.

How far a phone visitor has to travel to reach the buy button

Measured by loading each product page in a real browser at iPhone size (390×844) and reading the position of the first add-to-cart control, in screen-heights from the top of the page. Our own page was measured with the pop-up script blocked, so the number reflects the page itself.

What we measured on each product page

Three findings, and what they cost

Confirmed
A full-screen pop-up covers the product page at 0.0 seconds, before anything is readable.

Measured by polling every 250ms from page load: the overlay is visible at 0.0s, covers 100% of the phone screen, and locks scrolling. It asks “What are we helping you fix today?” and offers four categories — a question a visitor arriving from a Super Liver ad answered by clicking the ad.

Grüns and Cheers run the same pop-up tool and both let the visitor see the page first (2.25s and 1.5s). More Labs shows none at all. We are the only site in the set that interrupts on arrival.

On the reported A/B test: six cold sessions, six fresh browser profiles, all six got the immediate 100% overlay. That does not prove the test is not running — allocation may exclude automated traffic — but no variant was reachable from outside. Worth confirming in the Alia dashboard.

Confirmed — smaller gap than first reported
Our buy box sits 1.7 screens down; the comparison set sits at 0.8–0.95.

An earlier draft of this analysis put Grüns and The Absorption Company at “top of page.” That was wrong — it read a hidden sticky element as the buy box. Re-measured after properly dismissing overlays, they sit at 0.80 and 0.95 screens, and Cheers at 0.91.

So the gap is roughly double, not infinite. Still last in the set, and still meaningful on a phone — but the corrected number is the one to quote.

Confirmed
There is a sticky header with a cart icon, but no sticky add-to-cart bar.

On a page 17.2 screens tall, we checked at 35%, 50% and 90% depth with the pop-up script blocked so the page was fully visible. The only pinned full-width element is the navigation header. Its cart icon opens the cart drawer — it cannot add Super Liver to the cart. Cheers, Grüns and The Absorption Company all keep a working add-to-cart bar on screen at every depth.

The consequence: the strongest material on the page — per-ingredient research, the comparison table, the results timeline — sits where a reader who has just been convinced cannot act on it.

The component already exists. productStickyATC is present in nine product sections of the theme. main-product.liquid — the section Super Liver actually renders — is the only one without it.
